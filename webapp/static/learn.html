Why a Fourier transform catches AI images

A generative model does not paint pixels one at a time. It builds a small, coarse representation of the picture and then upsamples it, several times over, until it is full size. The filters that do that upsampling are learned weights, not mathematically ideal interpolators, and they are the same weights for every image the model ever makes. So every output carries a faint, repeating residue: a periodic pattern a fraction of one grey level deep.

In the pixel domain that residue is hopeless — a whisper spread over 250,000 pixels, far below the noise. The discrete Fourier transform changes the accounting. It collects everything that repeats at a given rate into a single coefficient, so a whisper spread over the whole image becomes a handful of bright dots at fixed coordinates. The detector never looks at the picture. It looks at those coordinates.

Upload an image and it is carried through the detector one stage at a time — the crop, the residual, the spectrum, the mask, the comparison, the score — with the real tensors drawn at every step, ending in the verdict from the seven trained checkpoints. Nothing on this page is simulated, and nothing is added to your image.

The default is the standard cameraman tile, used only so the page has something to draw before you upload. Steps 1 to 3 are interactive tools for reading a spectrum; Step 4 is the detector itself, running on your picture.

The vocabulary, in one panel

Four terms are used throughout. Nothing else is needed.

Spatial domain
The picture as you see it: a grid of brightness values. "Pixel (120, 40) is fairly dark."
Frequency domain
The same picture as a recipe of stripe patterns. "There is a strong pattern repeating every four pixels, running diagonally."
Bin
One entry in the frequency-domain grid — one specific stripe pattern. A 478×478 spectrum has 228,484 of them. The mask holds one number per bin.
Fingerprint
Energy that appears at the same bins in every image a given model makes, because it comes from that model's fixed weights rather than from the scene.

Reading the spectrum. Distance from the centre = how fast the stripes repeat: near the middle is slow and broad, out at the edge is a pattern that flips every two pixels. Direction from the centre = which way the stripes run. That is the whole vocabulary — every point in the right-hand picture is one stripe pattern, and its brightness is how much of that pattern the image contains.
Why two dots? The image is real-valued, so the spectrum is symmetric: whatever sits at (ky, kx) is mirrored at (−ky, −kx). Set both sliders to zero and the two dots merge in the middle — that single bin is called DC, and it is just the average brightness of the whole image.

Around 1% of the coefficients is already recognisable, and nothing was invented to get there — those numbers were always in the image. The DFT is a lossless change of basis: 65,536 pixels in, 65,536 coefficients out, and an exact inverse. This is also, in one sentence, why JPEG works.
Notice where the kept coefficients are (third panel): clustered tight around the centre. Photographic content is overwhelmingly low-frequency. Push the slider up and watch the new dots appear out towards the edges, adding fine texture and sharp edges — and almost nothing you would call "the picture".

The two signals separate by position. Image content varies wildly from photograph to photograph and sits near DC. A generator's fingerprint sits far out and is identical in every image that model makes. The Fourier transform puts them in different places — which is exactly what makes a static mask, one fixed number per bin, enough to tell them apart.
This is also the whole reason the detector denoises first. Pull the radius down to 5 and look at the left panel: still clearly the scene. The content is thousands of times stronger than anything a generator leaves behind, so it has to go before the comparison, or it drowns everything.

The real model, in numbers

What the checkpoints in this repository actually contain.

685,926mask values — 3 × 478 × 478, one per frequency bin, each squashed into [0, 1]
685,926reference values — the same shape again: what this generator's fingerprint is supposed to look like
11every other learned parameter: nine for the 1×1 colour convolution, two for the logistic
0convolutional feature extractors. There is no backbone, no ResNet, no transformer anywhere in the model
7separate detectors, one per generator — SD 1, SD 2, SD-XL, DALL·E 2, DALL·E 3, Midjourney, Firefly
≈ 12,000real photographs per generator in the calibration tables, from COCO and FlickrFaces
512 → 478crop size, then the DnCNN's 17-pixel invalid border removed from every edge
~3 hto train one detector on the paper's hardware. The expensive part is reading images, not the maths

What it cannot do

Worth saying out loud before anyone asks. None of these are bugs; they follow directly from the design.

Rescaling defeats it. The mask watches fixed coordinates, so the image must be at its original sampling rate. Resize by a few percent and the peaks land between the bins the mask is open at. Nearly every image that has passed through a social network has been resized. You can see this happen in the lab above.
It fails by going blind. An image from a generator no detector was trained on scores like a photograph. That is a false negative — it will never invent an accusation — which is the safe direction to fail in, but it is silent: nothing looks broken when it stops working.
"No evidence" ≠ "authentic". Each detector answers one narrow question: does this carry this particular generator's fingerprint? Seven "no"s mean seven specific models left no trace. They do not mean a camera took the picture.
Whole images only. Inpainted or partially-edited images are out of scope by the authors' own statement: the fingerprint covers only the regenerated region, and the global spectrum averages it away.
It is a decoder detector. It transfers between models that share a decoder architecture and not otherwise. Trained on SD 2 it scores 96.5 on SD-XL — same VAE family — but only 71.3 on DALL·E 2.
The spectrum has a known flaw. A DFT treats the image as if it wrapped around, so the mismatch between opposite edges adds a bright cross through the middle of every spectrum. The paper notes the fix (periodic-plus-smooth decomposition) and does not apply it.
